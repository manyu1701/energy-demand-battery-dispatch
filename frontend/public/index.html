Energy Demand Forecasting & Battery Dispatch

Forecast hourly grid demand and price, backtest the model across five regimes, then optimize battery charge/discharge against the forecast.

Hourly series

Synthetic but structured: annual and daily seasonality, holidays, autocorrelated residuals, renewable-driven negative prices and scarcity spikes. Served straight from the CSV the model was trained on.

Average day

Mean demand and price by hour of day across the whole year — the double peak the battery is built to arbitrage.

Daily price range

Daily min, mean and max. The gap between min and max is the spread a battery can capture; wide-spread days are where the money is.

Feature importance

Gain share, top 15 features. Lag and rolling features carry the persistence; calendar features carry the shape.

Error by hour of day

Mean absolute error per hour. A model can look fine on average and still be worst at the evening peak — which is exactly when the battery decides whether to discharge.

Five regimes, one model

A single train/test split tells you almost nothing — pick a lucky window and any model looks good. Each regime stresses a different failure mode. Skill is measured against a seasonal-naive baseline (same hour, last week); 0% means the model adds nothing over that.

Dispatch schedule — most volatile week

Bars are charging (positive) and discharging (negative); the line is the price the schedule was settled at. The battery buys the troughs and sells the peaks.

Cumulative profit

Running total over the test window. The gap between forecast-driven and perfect foresight is the cost of forecast error.

Storage duration sweep

Same 20 MWh of energy, different power ratings. More power earns more in total, but cycles the battery far harder for a narrower spread per cycle. Degradation cost is not modelled here — which is exactly why the highest-power option looks best, and why this table is a starting point for a sizing decision rather than the answer to it.
